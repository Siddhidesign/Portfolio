One order portal for 200 employees

01Input

Where every order started

Phone, paper, phone again

Every order took the same slow route. Staff were good at their jobs. The tools were the problem.

  1. A customer calls the store and asks for a size, colour or item.
  2. Staff write it in a book by hand, in a paper file.
  3. Staff call the supplier to place the order, one call at a time.
  4. Each person tracks their own orders, so nobody sees the full picture.

Slow

A whole day for only 2 or 3 orders.

Lost

Orders got misplaced. To know the status, you had to call someone.

Scattered

The rest of the work lived in 5 separate systems that did not talk to each other.

02Data in

Field research, before AI

About 200 people, every team

Our team of 3 designers met employees in all 10 stores before we drew a screen. Together we talked to about 200 people. I ran 80+ of those interviews myself, and watched how orders really move in the stores.

~200employees met across 10 stores, as a team
80+interviews I ran myself
6teams: sales, managers, purchasing, tailoring, HR, owner
What we heardMost of them had never used a work app. Many work on their feet, with a customer waiting.
03Analyze

Mapping, by hand

Map every task, and every handoff between teams

We mapped every team's tasks and every handoff, then gave each workflow one home. The structure went round twice: once while mapping, once after testing. All of this was done by hand, before AI tools were in use.

Staff work from phones

Not from a desk. Every screen needed a phone layout.

The caller is still waiting

An order can't stop halfway to look something up.

One order, typed many times

The same order went into more than one place, so copies didn't match.

04Verify · me

Testing with staff

Tested with staff, then audited after launch

We tested the prototype with staff and fed what we learned back into the design. After launch, we audited the live portal against 10 usability heuristics: each finding with its impact and a fix.

05Design

Decisions log

Four calls that shaped the portal

I owned the supplier-to-customer workflow: reorder from suppliers, take customer orders, and track them to delivery. I also built templates and components the team designed with, and worked with developers in Jira through the build.

Tesler's lawshipped

Enter an order once, follow it to the end

The work is complex, so the system carries it. Staff see one order moving through clear statuses: Pending, Approved, In transit, Delivered.

instead ofa separate place for each step

5 systems became 1.

Error preventionmeasured

Check stock before approving

Stock problems used to show up after an order was approved, when it had already moved. Now the stock check comes first.

instead ofapprove first, in one click

27% fewer order errors.

Miller's lawmeasured

A sales order in 4 small steps

Each step is short enough to finish while the customer waits, at the counter or on the phone.

instead ofone long form

40% faster per order.

User controlshipped

Add a new customer inside the order

On a phone order, the caller is on the line. So a new customer is added right there, without leaving the order.

instead ofa "No customer found" dead end

Part of the 4-step order.

I also worked on the marketing website's navigation, so shoppers can find a collection or a store fast, without a login wall.

Errors fell because no order gets lost now. Each one is recorded once, has an owner and a status, and can be followed from the customer's call to delivery.

Workflows are people

The slow parts were the handoffs between teams, which no single team could see.

Design for the least sure user

Big buttons and plain words helped people who had never used a work app.

Measure what the business feels

Orders a day, time to record and errors were the numbers everyone cared about.

In 2026 I rebuilt the whole workflow in Figma with sample data, with AI help and reviewed by me: 26 flows across every role, from sign-in to day-end closing, including onboarding, voice search and WCAG 2.2 AA. The live portal is internal, so this example shows how far the thinking goes.
