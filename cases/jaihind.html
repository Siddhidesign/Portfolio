01Departed from

Phone, paper, phone again

Every order took the same slow route. Staff were good at their jobs. The tools were the problem.

The old route of one order

  1. A customer calls the storeand asks for a size, colour or item.
  2. Staff write it in a bookby hand, in a paper file.
  3. Staff call the supplierto place the order, one call at a time.
  4. Each person tracks their own ordersso nobody sees the full picture.

What that cost

  • SlowA whole day for only 2 or 3 orders.
  • LostOrders got misplaced. To know the status, you had to call someone.
  • WrongHandwritten sizes and repeated calls led to wrong items and missed follow-ups.
  • ScatteredThe rest of the work lived in 5 separate systems that did not talk to each other.

04Turbulence

What the design had to handle

These came straight from the store visits.

  • On the floor

    Staff work from phones

    Not from a desk. Every screen needed a phone layout.

  • On the phone

    The caller is still waiting

    An order can't stop halfway to look something up.

  • In the system

    One order, typed many times

    The same order went into more than one place, so copies didn't match.

05Key decisions

Four calls that shaped the portal

Each one with the option we passed on, and the UX law behind it.

  1. Tesler's law

    Enter an order once, follow it to the end

    The work is complex, so the system carries it. Staff see one order moving through clear statuses: Pending, Approved, In transit, Delivered.

    Instead of: a separate place for each step.

    Shipped5 systems became 1.

  2. Error prevention

    Check stock before approving

    Stock problems used to show up after an order was approved, when it had already moved. Now the stock check comes first.

    Instead of: approve first, in one click.

    Measured27% fewer order errors.

  3. Miller's law

    A sales order in 4 small steps

    Each step is short enough to finish while the customer waits, at the counter or on the phone.

    Instead of: one long form.

    Measured40% faster per order.

  4. User control

    Add a new customer inside the order

    On a phone order, the caller is on the line. So a new customer is added right there, without leaving the order.

    Instead of: a "No customer found" dead end.

    ShippedPart of the 4-step order.

09Next flight

If I did it today, with AI

An idea for the next version. Not built yet.

Concept · AI agent

An agent drafts reorders, staff approve

It reads stock and sales, drafts the supplier order, and flags conflicts, like the same size asked for by two stores. A person always has the final say.

How I'd keep it trustworthy

  • Small tasks alone. Money and orders only with approval.
  • Every draft says why: "Sold 14 this week, 3 left."
  • Undo, and a log of everything it did.
  • Hand off to a person when it isn't sure.

What I learned

  • Workflows are people

    The slow parts were the handoffs between teams, which no single team could see.

  • Design for the least sure user

    Big buttons and plain words helped people who had never used a work app.

  • Measure what the business feels

    Orders a day, time to record and errors were the numbers everyone cared about.
