1Step 1 of 6 · Research · Heuristic review, proto-personas, journey maps

Pain point 1: one order, typed five times

Jai Hind Group has sold menswear in Maharashtra since 1980. It serves two audiences from two sides of the counter.

  • Shoppers needed to find a collection or a store, fast.
  • Staff typed the same order into 5 systems that didn't talk to each other.

Why it matters: every retype was another chance to get the order wrong. Both halves serve the same orders, so I designed for both.

Pain point 2: the console did the right jobs, badly

Later, in a self-directed redesign, I audited the live website (21 pages) and the team's dashboard (26 screens) against 10 usability heuristics.

  • 3 high: it didn't look like Jai Hind, there were no phone layouts for staff on the shop floor, and the order status tabs ran off the screen.
  • 4 medium: the cart showed 0 items while a product said "1 added," totals didn't add up, "Edit" opened a panel called "Create Customer," and sample data came from another region.
  • 3 low: a disabled checkout with no hint, no log out, and rows too alike to scan.

Insight: the screens covered the right jobs. The problems were brand, phones, status visibility and data that didn't add up. So I fixed what was broken instead of redesigning for its own sake.

Who I designed for, and where it hurts

Three proto-personas framed the work. They are assumptions to test with real shoppers and staff, not findings.

  • Rohan, the occasion shopper. Phone first. Wants to see what's new and find the nearest store.
  • Priya, a store manager. Desktop at the counter, phone on the floor. Wants to approve and dispatch fast, and spot problem orders.
  • Amit, a floor associate. Phone only. Places an order while the customer waits.

Journey maps showed two peaks: shoppers drop off when they try to work out which store carries a brand, and staff stress peaks on phone orders, with the caller waiting on the line.

2Step 2 of 6 · Define · Problem statements, information architecture, user flows

Two problem statements, one structure

Problem 1. Shoppers know a brand name, not the company behind it, so they need to find a collection or a store without knowing how the business is organized.

How might we organize the website the way shoppers ask: what do you sell, and where are you?

Problem 2. Staff re-enter the same order in 5 systems, and later fight a console where status, stock and totals are hard to trust.

How might we let staff enter an order once and always see where it is?

5Step 5 of 6 · Design · Design system, hi-fi screens, prototype

Seven changes, and the principle behind each

Change 1 shipped and was measured. Changes 2 to 7 are my self-directed redesign: I pulled Jai Hind's colors, fonts and photos into a small design system (37 colors, 17 text styles, 13 components, 10 status colors), redesigned all 26 screens for desktop and phone, and linked 118 screens with 2,104 interactions in Figma.

  1. Change 1 · Staff · Shipped

    Enter the order once

    One platform, shared by 3 business functions, replaced 5 disconnected systems. I turned what those functions needed into 30+ documented requirements and user flows. An order is entered once and moves through every status in one place. I also made the templates and component library that 3 designers and a PM designed against, and worked with the developers through implementation so the design carried into production.

    UX principles

    • Error prevention. If nobody retypes an order, nobody can retype it wrong.
    • Visibility of system status. Every order's status lives in one place.

    Measured27% fewer user errors and 40% more efficient workflows after launch.

  2. Change 2 · Both

    One front door for both

    The staff tools lived at a separate address and looked nothing like the brand. Now both doors sit behind one welcome screen: "Explore the store" for shoppers, "Employee login" for staff.

    UX principles

    • Recognition over recall. Two labeled doors, no need to remember a separate address.
    • User control and freedom. Shoppers browse without an account. Log out returns staff to Welcome.
  3. Change 3 · Staff

    Borrow the brand, don't invent one

    Shoppers saw the brand; staff saw generic teal at a desk. The console now uses the website's palette, fonts, a black header and navy silk banners.

    UX principles

    • Consistency and standards. The same company on both sides of the counter.
    • Aesthetic and minimalist design. A small system: every fill bound to one of 37 color variables.
  4. Change 4 · Staff

    Every screen works on a phone

    The console was desktop only, but Priya walks the floor and Amit only has his phone. All 26 screens got a phone layout: cards, bottom sheets, a tab bar and a sticky cart bar.

    UX principles

    • Flexibility and efficiency of use. The same job, wherever staff are standing.
    • Fitts's law. A bottom tab bar and sticky cart bar, within thumb reach.
  5. Change 5 · Staff

    Every order status in view

    Status tabs ran off the screen. Now all tabs sit in the page banner, each with a count, and become scrollable pills on a phone. Each status keeps one color everywhere.

    UX principles

    • Visibility of system status. Counts show where work is piling up.
    • Consistency and standards. One color per status in tabs, tables, dialogs and charts.
  6. Change 6 · Staff

    Check stock before approving

    Stock problems used to surface only after approving. Now Approve runs an inventory check first. If an item is short, staff see it before the order moves, and can split it into a sub-order.

    UX principles

    • Error prevention. The problem shows up before the decision, not after.
  7. Change 7 · Staff

    Phone orders without dead ends

    "No customer found" was a dead end while the caller waited. Now staff add the customer right there. One live cart keeps the same count everywhere, a 4-step indicator shows progress, and totals add up on every change.

    UX principles

    • Help users recover from errors. Not found leads to Add customer, not a stop.
    • Visibility of system status. One cart state and a step indicator.
    • Error prevention. Totals recalculated on every step.

    Not yet testedA clickable prototype, not yet tested with staff.

6Step 6 of 6 · Measure · Post-launch metrics, test plan

What's proven, and how I'd test the rest

The order platform was measured after launch. The website numbers are scope, not results: I never measured its traffic. The redesign is untested, so these are targets:

  • Under 2 minutes to take a phone order with three items
  • 90% of managers spot flagged orders on the first click
  • 3 taps or fewer from Welcome to store directions
  • 0 wrong orders when updating five at once

Test plan: moderated sessions with 5 to 6 store staff (managers, billing, floor associates) and 5 shoppers.

What I learned

  • Follow the question

    Shoppers ask what you sell and where you are. Staff ask where an order is. Nobody asks which system holds it.

  • Say it once

    Five copies of one order meant five chances to get it wrong. Collections need the same rule: one name, everywhere.

  • It's one company

    Shoppers saw the brand; staff didn't. A business should feel like itself on both sides of the counter.

What I'd do differently now

Ideas for a next version, not built yet.

A store helper for shoppers

Ask "which store has this blazer in my size?" and get an answer from live stock, with directions.

Orders from a photo or a voice note
