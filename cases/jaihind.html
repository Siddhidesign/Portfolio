1Step 1 of 7 · Problem

One business, two audiences

Jai Hind Group has sold menswear in Maharashtra since 1980. Shoppers needed to find a collection or a store, fast. Behind the counter, staff typed the same order into 5 systems that didn't talk to each other.

Why: Both halves serve the same orders from two sides of the counter, so I designed for both.

2Step 2 of 7 · Website

Organize the site the way shoppers ask

Shoppers arrive with two questions: what do you sell, and where are you? I designed the website's structure to answer both.

Key decision
  1. Key decision 1

    Three ways in: brand, collection, store

    The thinking: Shoppers know a brand name, not the company behind it. So the site has three ways in: 2 brands, 8 collections named the way people shop, like Formal or Ethnic, and all 10 stores in 4 cities.

    ShippedShipped. The site was rebuilt after I left; this structure is my work. Not measured.

3Step 3 of 7 · Platform

Stop typing the same order five times

For staff, I designed the order platform that replaced 5 disconnected systems, plus the templates and component library that 3 designers and a PM worked from.

Key decision
  1. Key decision 2

    Enter the order once

    The thinking: Every retype was another chance to get it wrong. One platform, shared by 3 business functions, lets staff enter an order once and move it through every status in one place.

    Measured27% fewer user errors and 40% more efficient workflows after launch.

4Step 4 of 7 · Research

Audit what was there

Later, in a self-directed redesign, I studied the live website (21 pages) and the team's dashboard (26 screens), then checked the dashboard against 10 usability rules.

  • The screens covered the right jobs. The problems were the brand, phones, order status, and totals that didn't add up.
  • Three proto-personas, a shopper, a store manager and a floor associate, framed the work. They're assumptions to test, not findings.

Why: Auditing first meant fixing what was broken, not redesigning for its own sake.

5Step 5 of 7 · Ideas

Eight ideas in eight minutes

I sketched 8 ideas in 8 minutes for how shoppers and staff should get in. Then I wireframed every key screen in gray, desktop and phone side by side.

  • Kept: two equal doors on one welcome screen.
  • Dropped: a login wall, since shoppers shouldn't need an account to browse.
  • In the wireframes, a sales order became 4 clear stages, with the cart always in view.

Why: Paper is cheap to throw away, and gray wireframes keep the talk on layout, not color.

6Step 6 of 7 · Design

Borrow the brand, then build every screen

I pulled Jai Hind's colors, fonts and photos from its website into a small design system: 37 colors, 17 text styles, 13 components and 10 order status colors. Then I redesigned all 26 screens for desktop and phone, and linked 118 screens with 2,104 interactions in Figma.

Key decision
  1. Key decision 3

    One front door for both

    The thinking: The staff tools lived at a separate address and looked nothing like the brand. In my redesign, both doors sit behind one welcome screen, in Jai Hind's own look.

7Step 7 of 7 · Results

What's proven, and what's next

The website numbers are scope, not results: I never measured its traffic. The redesign is untested; next, I'd test it with 5 to 6 store staff and 5 shoppers.

  • 27%fewer user errors, measured
  • 40%more efficient workflows, measured

What I learned

  • Follow the question

    Shoppers ask what you sell and where you are. Staff ask where an order is. Nobody asks which system holds it.

  • Say it once

    Five copies of one order meant five chances to get it wrong. Collections need the same rule: one name, everywhere.

  • It's one company

    Shoppers saw the brand; staff didn't. A business should feel like itself on both sides of the counter.

If I did it today, with AI

What I'd do differently now

Ideas for a next version, not built yet.

A store helper for shoppers

Ask "which store has this blazer in my size?" and get an answer from live stock, with directions.

Orders from a photo or a voice note

Staff snap a handwritten order or read it out, and AI fills in the form for them to check. Less typing, fewer mistakes.
