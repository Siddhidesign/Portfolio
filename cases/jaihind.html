In 30 seconds

The short version

Jai Hind is a Pune fashion retailer with 10 stores. Shoppers find the brand online. Store staff take walk-in and phone orders in an internal dashboard.

The problem

Two products that looked like two companies

The staff dashboard looked nothing like the brand, only worked at a desk, hid order statuses off screen and caught stock problems too late.

What I did

Research to prototype

I audited both, sketched ideas, made wireframes, built a style guide from the brand, then redesigned all 26 staff screens for desktop and phone.

The result

One front door

A clickable prototype that joins the shop website and the staff dashboard behind one welcome screen, ready to test with store staff.

Before and after

Same jobs. A much better dashboard.

Three proto-personas

Assumption-based profiles that guided the design. They are hypotheses to test with real customers and store staff, not research findings.

R
Rohan, 27

The occasion shopper

CustomerProto-persona · assumption

Lives in Pune. Shops for weddings, festivals and office wear. Finds brands on Instagram and Google Maps.

Goals
  • See what’s new before visiting a store
  • Find the nearest store and get directions
  • Browse by occasion: ethnic, formal, blazers and suits
Frustrations
  • Hard to tell which store carries Jaihind or Mewar
  • Long pages on a phone before reaching store links
Needs from the design
  • One tap from Welcome to the store
  • Collections and store links in the menu and footer
Devices: Phone first, sometimes laptop
P
Priya, 34

Store manager, Laxmi Road

EmployeeProto-persona · assumption

Runs daily orders for walk-in and phone customers. Works at the billing counter on desktop and walks the floor with her phone.

Goals
  • Approve and dispatch orders quickly
  • Spot flagged, delayed or understocked orders
  • Look up a customer’s history before calling them
Frustrations
  • Status tabs and wide tables that don’t fit the screen
  • Tools that don’t work on a phone
  • Stock problems discovered only after approving
Needs from the design
  • Status at a glance with counts
  • Bulk status change and an inventory check before approving
  • The same screens on mobile
Devices: Desktop at the counter, plus phone
A
Amit, 23

Floor associate

EmployeeProto-persona · assumption

Helps customers find sizes. Places an order on his phone when an item is out of stock in the store.

Goals
  • Find a customer by phone number in seconds
  • Add products and place the order without losing the customer
  • Tell the customer when to expect delivery
Frustrations
  • Small tap targets and long forms
  • No way to see the cart while adding items
Needs from the design
  • Bottom tab bar and a sticky cart bar
  • A step indicator through the order
  • Short, one-column forms

02

Sketches: explore ideas fast, on paper

I sketched 8 ideas in 8 minutes for how shoppers and staff should get in, then sketched every key screen before opening Figma.

  • Winner: two equal doors on one welcome screen.
  • Dropped: a login wall for everyone. Shoppers should never need an account just to browse.
  • Dropped: a tiny staff link in the footer. Too easy to miss.
03

Wireframes: get the layout right in gray first

Gray boxes only, desktop and phone side by side, with numbered notes that explain each decision. No colors yet, so the conversation stays on structure.

  • Dashboard: key numbers first, then trends, then top products and customers.
  • Sales order: 4 clear stages (Customer, Products, Delivery and payment, Checkout), with the cart always in view.

Final design: 26 screens, desktop and phone

Every screen from the team's dashboard, redesigned at desktop and phone sizes, plus the new welcome and login screens. The work staff already knew stayed the same; the friction went away.

Take a phone order

06

Prototype: click through it like the real thing

In Figma I connected 118 screens with 2,104 interactions into one prototype, with separate desktop and phone flows. Beside this is the phone version rebuilt in code: same screens, a tab bar, cards instead of tables and a sticky cart bar.

  • Welcome, then Explore the store, opens the shop website.
  • Welcome, then Employee login, opens the dashboard, orders, customers and the 10-step sales order.
  • Log out always brings you back to Welcome.

What I learned

Three things I'd tell my past self

1

Audit your own work with fresh eyes

The platform worked, and a simple audit still found 10 problems.

2

Design for the shop floor, not just the desk

Staff walk the floor with phones, so every screen now has a phone layout.

3

Label guesses as guesses

Personas and targets stay marked as assumptions until real users test them.
